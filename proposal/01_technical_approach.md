Qu-SAFE's barrier is not physics. NV-diamond magnetometers already read bioelectric signals from live mammalian tissue non-invasively at room temperature, and optically pumped magnetometry, quantum dot labels, and single-photon detection each reach below the classical floor. What does not exist is the bridge into preclinical toxicology: a cardiomyocyte field potential a microelectrode array records only as a population average, or a hepatotoxicity marker under the limit of detection of the plate assay it would replace. Three things block that bridge. Quantum physicists, bioengineers, and preclinical toxicologists are not in the same room, a network gap. Sensors have to integrate into the microphysiological systems labs already run, an engineering gap. Nobody has shown a validated quantum advantage in standardized preclinical safety testing, an evidence gap. Our stage-gated approach closes them in that order.

FedTech runs this challenge inside NCATS's Qu-BIT portfolio, alongside TraCE MPS moving tissue chips toward FDA qualification and MATChS automating them for scale; ISTAND is where a sensor-enhanced chip would seek qualification. The Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine (WFIRM) is subcontracted for NAMs and toxicology judgment. NASA and NIH have already flown this science together: AVATAR flew NCATS-partnered organ chips around the Moon on Artemis II in April 2026, and those samples are in analysis now.

Challenge Design and Stage Structure: Before launch FedTech works with NCATS and NASA BPS to confirm objectives and the project plan, and develops all competition rules, terms, and conditions for approval. We review NIH's published criteria and recommend refinements before each round opens, with final criteria Government-approved. We establish modality-specific benchmarks, so a team proposing NV-diamond magnetometry is assessed against magnetometry sensitivity standards and a team proposing photon counting against photon-counting standards, rather than both against one generic rubric.

Stage 1 (Ideation) is optional, but clearing Stage 2 Milestone 1 is mandatory to reach Milestone 2. Stage 1 therefore works as a recruiting and team-formation instrument rather than a gate. Every team declares a preclinical use case and a target signal before any build, which is what makes a physicist's claim reviewable by a toxicologist. Up to 25 winners advance.

Between Stage 1 and Milestone 1, matchmaking is a deliverable rather than a courtesy. Registrants declare their modality, their target signal, and what they lack; FedTech pairs across that gap and runs sessions where new teams pressure-test a use case before committing to it. We track the registered pool against what the round needs and flag where one discipline is thin, so recruitment goes where the pipeline is short. Registered teams can draw on PhysioVerse, the open-source MPS data ecosystem WFIRM helps run, subject to NCATS and BPS agreement.

Milestone 1 (Initial Progress) opens to Stage 1 winners and to new entrants on equal terms, with both populations tracked separately so NCATS and BPS can see whether Stage 1 measurably improved entry quality. It requires the sensor integrated into 2D cultures and reference fluids a receiving lab already uses, and a working data path, not a demonstration in the team's own rig. Up to 15 winners advance.

Milestone 2 (Final Prototype) closes the evidence gap: a 3D organoid demonstration benchmarked against the classical method it would replace, carrying a prototype data package, recorded test runs, and a live session. FedTech runs them through virtual broadcasts and standardized data packages, so no team ships hardware or is disadvantaged by distance from a receiving lab. Scoring weights calibration, limit of detection against the claimed endpoint, and reproducibility beyond a single lab. One grand prize winner and up to six runners-up.

Outreach and Participant Recruitment: A combined quantum-sensing and preclinical-toxicology solver community does not exist yet, so we recruit against two named lists rather than broadcast to one. On the quantum side: American Physical Society meetings, National Quantum Initiative centers, and the DOE quantum information science centers - Q-NEXT and C2QA at Argonne and Brookhaven, where FedTech holds active conversations, and Sandia. On the NAMs and toxicology side: the Society of Toxicology, the NCATS tissue chip grantee community, and the NAMs standards meeting WFIRM convenes each December. We recruit at HBCUs, MSIs, and EPSCoR institutions and report participation as a metric rather than asserting it.

Platform and Submission Management: The competition website runs on a custom, non-FedTech-branded URL carrying NIH and NASA identity, taking document and multimedia submissions, managing each submission window, and hosting the public forum. It meets Section 508 and 36 C.F.R. Part 1194, audited before each round opens rather than after launch. The judging record is retained at least a year past task order end. FedTech is the single point of contact for competitor questions and disputes.

Judging and Evaluation: A Technical Evaluation Panel scores each submission against NIH's published criteria. Quantum-sensing specialists take technical approach and workflow; NAMs and toxicology specialists take measurement and characterization, data analysis, and innovation and impact. Stage 1 submissions get three reviews, one quantum and two biomedical; each Stage 2 milestone gets four, two and two. Where the panels split, FedTech convenes both chairs before recommendations reach the federal Judging Panel: a submission the hardware panel rates highly and the biology panel rates poorly is the failure mode this challenge exists to catch, and averaging would hide it. The quantum bench comes from the network FedTech built under NOIS3-039, recruited and compensated through QED-C and those centers. Anthony Atala, James Yoo, and Shay Soker of WFIRM staff the NAMs and toxicology panel. External reviewers are cleared against the FAR 52.225-5 designated country list, submissions are screened for eligibility and scope before reaching a judge, and an SME who intends to compete in a later stage recuses from judging it.

In-Person Convening: FedTech hosts a day-and-a-half convening for 75 to 100 winners, runners-up, and advisors in the DC and Bethesda area near the NIH campus. Winners present to venture investors, SBIR program staff, and other funders FedTech brings into the room. FedTech drafts the agenda and run-of-show, handles venue, registration, and staffing, sends winners and runners-up travel logistics, covers its own staff travel, and delivers the post-event report. WFIRM shapes the technical content and moderates.

Reporting and Network Handoff: FedTech completes the USA.gov posting, drafts the participation agreement accepted at registration (government indemnification, $250,000 in liability insurance or demonstrated financial responsibility, participant-retained intellectual property), and verifies each winner's US standing and federal-employee status before prize certification. Across five years with long gaps between milestones, quarterly office hours, two technical webinars a year, and a standing newsletter keep the community from going cold. At closeout NASA and NIH receive the network itself: a roster of specialists who would not otherwise have worked together, the engagement history behind it, and a recommended stewardship model.
