NASA and NIH have already run one investigation into what happens to the human body under conditions no lab on Earth can recreate. AVATAR, led by NASA with BARDA and NCATS, flew organ chips carrying bone marrow tissue grown from Artemis II crew cells around the Moon in April 2026, the first use of organ chips beyond low Earth orbit, and those samples are in analysis now. Qu-SAFE asks a related question: whether sensing physics built for space and defense can give tissue chips the sensitivity they have lacked in ordinary labs.

Qu-SAFE sits inside a wider NCATS push on the same measurement bottleneck. TraCE MPS is moving tissue chips toward FDA qualification, MATChS is automating them for scale, and the Tissue Chip for Drug Screening Program built the grantee base all three draw on. Qu-SAFE approaches the same gap through sensing physics; FDA's ISTAND pathway is where a sensor-enhanced chip would seek qualification. FedTech runs this challenge inside NCATS's Qu-BIT portfolio, with the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine (WFIRM) subcontracted for NAMs and toxicology judgment.

The sensing physics is already proven. NV-diamond magnetometers read bioelectric signals from live mammalian tissue non-invasively at room temperature, and optically pumped magnetometry, quantum dot labels, and single-photon detection each reach below the floor classical readouts hit. What is missing is the bridge into preclinical toxicology workflows: a cardiomyocyte field potential a microelectrode array records only as a population average, or a hepatotoxicity marker sitting under the limit of detection of the plate assay it would replace.

Three things block that bridge. Quantum physicists, bioengineers, and preclinical toxicologists are not in the same room, a network gap. Sensors have to integrate into the microphysiological systems and microfluidic rigs labs already run, an engineering gap. Nobody has shown a validated quantum advantage in standardized preclinical safety testing, an evidence gap.

Dual-track recruitment and cross-disciplinary matchmaking address the network gap. Stage 1 takes on the engineering gap: every team declares a preclinical use case and a target signal before any build. Milestone 1 then requires the sensor integrated into 2D cultures and reference fluids a receiving lab already uses. Milestone 2 closes the evidence gap with 3D organoid demonstrations benchmarked against the classical method they would replace. FedTech plans and runs those demonstrations through virtual broadcasts, recorded test runs, standardized data packages, and panel Q&A, so no team ships hardware.

A combined quantum-sensing and preclinical-toxicology solver community does not exist yet. Outreach runs on two tracks at once. On the quantum side: American Physical Society meetings, National Quantum Initiative centers, and DOE quantum information science centers. On the NAMs and toxicology side: the Society of Toxicology, the NCATS tissue chip grantee community, and the NAMs standards meeting WFIRM convenes each December. FedTech recruits at HBCUs, MSIs, and EPSCoR institutions and reports participation as a metric rather than asserting it.

FedTech holds active conversations with the quantum teams at Argonne and Brookhaven, which lead the DOE centers Q-NEXT and C2QA, and at Sandia National Laboratories. The University of Maryland Quantum Startup Foundry accelerator cohort FedTech ran in 2022 reaches the university side.

Between launch and each deadline, FedTech tracks the registered pool and flags where one discipline is thin. Registrants declare their modality, their target signal, and what they lack. FedTech pairs across that gap and runs pairing sessions where new teams pressure-test a use case before committing to it. Registered teams can also draw on PhysioVerse, the open-source microphysiological systems data ecosystem WFIRM helps run, subject to NCATS and NASA BPS agreement.

A Technical Evaluation Panel scores each submission against NIH's published criteria, refined with NCATS and NASA BPS before each round. Quantum-sensing specialists take technical approach and workflow. NAMs and toxicology specialists take measurement and characterization, data analysis, and innovation and impact. Stage 1 submissions get three reviews, one quantum and two biomedical. Each Stage 2 milestone gets four, two and two. FedTech checks every submission against every criterion before scoring closes and returns gaps to the reviewer. Where the panels split, FedTech convenes both chairs to resolve it before recommendations reach the federal Judging Panel. The quantum panel comes from the network FedTech built under NOIS3-039, a NASA ESTO quantum computing benchmark whose landscape scan catalogued about 140 organizations. That interview pool included Celia Merzbacher of the Quantum Economic Development Consortium, Dana Anderson of Infleqtion, Johannes Galatsanos of Diffraqtion, and NVIDIA's quantum team. FedTech recruits and compensates the quantum reviewers through QED-C and those centers. Anthony Atala, James Yoo, and Shay Soker of WFIRM staff the NAMs and toxicology panel under subcontract, bringing the sensor-integration judgment to tell a real sensitivity gain from a well-presented one. Submissions are screened for eligibility and scope before reaching a judge. External technical reviewers are cleared against the FAR 52.225-5 designated country list before selection. An SME who intends to compete in a later stage recuses from judging it, a rule set with NCATS and NASA BPS before the round opens.

Working with NCATS and NASA BPS, FedTech develops the rules and terms and completes the USA.gov posting. The competition website runs on a custom, non-FedTech-branded URL taking document and multimedia submissions and managing each submission window. The judging record is kept a year past task order end. All products, platforms, and communications meet Section 508 and 36 C.F.R. Part 1194. Before prize certification FedTech verifies each winner's US standing and federal-employee status. FedTech drafts the participation agreement accepted at registration, covering government indemnification, $250,000 in liability insurance or demonstrated financial responsibility, and participant-retained intellectual property, and is the single point of contact for competitor questions and disputes.

The period of performance runs more than five years with long gaps between milestones. FedTech keeps the community active with quarterly office hours, two technical webinars a year, and a standing newsletter, so it does not go cold and need a relaunch at each stage.

The in-person convening brings winners, runners-up, and advisors together for a day and a half, 75 to 100 attendees in the Washington DC and Bethesda area near the NIH campus. Winners present to venture investors, SBIR program staff, and other funders FedTech brings into the room. FedTech drafts the agenda, run-of-show, and staffing plan, handles venue and registration, sends winners and runners-up travel logistics, covers its own staff travel, and delivers the post-event report. WFIRM shapes the technical content and moderates.

FedTech documents the SME and stakeholder network the challenge builds, quantum-sensing and NAMs and toxicology specialists who would not otherwise have worked together. NASA and NIH get a closeout package: a network roster, the engagement history behind it, and a recommended stewardship model for keeping it running.
