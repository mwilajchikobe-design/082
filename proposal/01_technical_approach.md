Qu-SAFE's barrier is not physics. Quantum biosensing already works in controlled settings — NV-diamond sensors detect bioelectric signals from live mammalian tissue non-invasively at room temperature. What does not exist is the bridge from that work to preclinical toxicology: standardized calibration, reproducibility beyond a single lab, benchmarking against classical methods, and validation by reviewers fluent in the hardware and the biology at once. The RFTP names this directly as interdisciplinary silos. FedTech designs this challenge to close that gap on real toxicity endpoints and to leave behind a durable cross-domain network NCATS and BPS can keep using after the task order closes. That outcome — an ecosystem, not an administered event — governs every choice below.

CHALLENGE DESIGN AND STAGE STRUCTURE. We run the competition as the RFTP structures it, and we say the eligibility rule plainly to solvers from day one: Stage 1 (Ideation) is optional, but clearing Stage 2 Milestone 1 (Initial Progress) is mandatory to reach Milestone 2 (Final Prototype). Stage 1 therefore functions as a recruiting and team-formation instrument rather than a gate. We use it to surface concepts and broker introductions between quantum-sensing groups and MPS/tissue-chip groups that would not otherwise meet — teams formed at Stage 1 are the most likely source of credible Milestone 1 entries. Milestone 1 opens to Stage 1 participants and to new entrants on equal terms, with the two populations tracked separately on the platform so NIH and NASA can see whether Stage 1 measurably improved entry quality. Milestone 1 requires an integrated sensor-plus-platform build and a working data path; Milestone 2 requires a benchmarked demonstration against a defined preclinical endpoint (hepatic, cardiac, neuro, or DART) with a classical comparator and a documented quantum-plus-classical pipeline. Each stage's rubric and scoring weights are approved by the COR before the stage opens.

COMPETITION WEBSITE AND PLATFORM. We stand up a dedicated challenge site on a custom URL that carries NIH/NCATS and NASA/BPS identity, not contractor branding, hosted on our established challenge platform. It handles registration, eligibility screening, versioned submissions, reviewer assignment, blinded scoring, and audit-ready records of every decision. All products, platform pages, and communications meet Section 508 requirements under 36 C.F.R. § 1194.1 and Appendices A, C, and D, verified by an accessibility audit before each stage opens, not after launch.

OUTREACH AND RECRUITMENT. The solver pool for this challenge is small, identifiable, and already partly known — NIH's own related quantum-sensing challenge under the same parent program surfaced credible academic and small-company teams whose work is public. We recruit against a named list, not a broadcast. Quantum channels: APS Division of Quantum Information, IEEE Sensors, Optica, National Quantum Initiative centers, and university quantum-sensing labs. Biology and toxicology channels: the Society of Toxicology, organ-on-chip and MPS consortia, NCATS' own tissue-chip community, and NASA/BPS investigators. Translation channels: university technology-transfer offices and the SBIR/STTR community, where the small instrumentation companies capable of building a prototype actually live. We commit to targeted recruitment at HBCUs, MSIs, and EPSCoR institutions, tracked and reported as a metric rather than asserted. Because the hardest recruiting problem is pairing a physicist with a toxicologist, matchmaking is an explicit outreach product: office hours, a partner-finding board, and two virtual mixers per stage.

COMPETITOR MANAGEMENT. FedTech is the sole point of contact for every competitor question, eligibility determination, and dispute. We maintain a published FAQ on a fixed cadence, log every inquiry with response time, and escalate only policy questions to the COR. Disputes are resolved against the published rules under a documented process that leaves a defensible record.

TECHNICAL REVIEW, FEDERAL JUDGING, AND SME VALIDATION. This is the requirement with no precedent in a standard challenge and the one we build the review architecture around. The SOW requires hiring SMEs able to validate quantum-sensing hardware and preclinical toxicology/NAMs science. A single generic technical panel cannot do this: a physicist cannot judge whether a hepatotoxicity endpoint is meaningful, and a toxicologist cannot judge whether a claimed sensitivity gain is real. We therefore run two coordinated review tracks scoring the same submissions against different rubric categories. The quantum and hardware track scores sensor performance, integration, calibration, noise characterization, and the credibility of any claimed quantum advantage. The toxicology and NAMs track scores endpoint relevance, biological validity, model fidelity, and translational value against existing preclinical practice. Both tracks score independently and blind, then meet in a moderated reconciliation session where divergent scores are surfaced and discussed rather than averaged away — a submission the hardware track rates highly and the biology track rates poorly is exactly the failure mode this challenge exists to catch, and averaging would hide it. FedTech moderates; the reconciliation record goes to the federal judges with the scores. Federal judges make all award determinations. External technical reviewers and SMEs advise and score only. We apply the FAR 52.225-5 designated-country restriction to the full external review population, including the paid SME panel, as a prudent default rather than reading it narrowly. All reviewers clear conflict-of-interest screening against the entrant list each stage, sign NDAs, and complete rubric training before scoring opens.

IN-PERSON CONVENING. We host a 1.5-day convening for 75-100 attendees in the Washington, DC/Bethesda area, chosen for proximity to NIH and the federal stakeholders whose engagement determines whether these prototypes go anywhere. Winners present their work; the rest of the agenda is built for translation — structured sessions with SBIR/STTR program staff, investors active in quantum and life sciences, and potential adopters. FedTech covers venue, audiovisual, and contractor staff travel. Participant travel is not budgeted, consistent with the RFTP. The convening is also where the cross-domain network built over the preceding stages becomes visible and transferable to the Government.

DOCUMENTATION, REPORTING, AND POST-AWARD EVALUATION. Rules, participation agreements, and submission terms state that intellectual property remains with the participant, that participants indemnify the Government, and that liability is capped as the SOW specifies. We deliver monthly progress reports, stage-close reports, and a final report. The RFTP's schedule is internally inconsistent — one section expects solutions available by May 2030, another requires all milestones complete by December 31, 2031. We resolve it in the Government's favor rather than assume the earlier date: the 12-month post-award evaluation survey follows the final Milestone 2 winner announcement, and we carry evaluation, network handoff, and closeout reporting explicitly through the end of the period of performance. The handoff deliverable is the SME roster, reviewer network, and stakeholder contacts built during the task order, transferred to NCATS and BPS for continued use.
