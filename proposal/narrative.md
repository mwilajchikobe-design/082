### 5.2.2 — Technical Approach (7,497 / 7,500)

Qu-SAFE's barrier is not physics. Nanodiamond sensors already read bioelectric signals from live mammalian tissue non-invasively at room temperature, and quantum magnetometry, quantum-enhanced optics and spectroscopy, and intracellular thermometry each reach below the classical floor. What does not exist is the bridge into preclinical toxicology: a cardiomyocyte field potential a microelectrode array records only as a population average, or a hepatotoxicity marker under the limit of detection of the plate assay it would replace. Three things block that bridge. Quantum physicists, bioengineers, and preclinical toxicologists are not in the same room, a network gap. Sensors have to integrate into the microphysiological systems labs already run, an engineering gap. Nobody has shown a validated quantum advantage in standardized preclinical safety testing, an evidence gap.

FedTech runs this challenge inside NCATS's Qu-BIT portfolio, with the Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine (WFIRM) subcontracted for NAMs and toxicology judgment; ISTAND is where a sensor-enhanced chip would seek qualification.

Challenge Design and Stage Structure: Before launch FedTech works with NCATS and NASA BPS to confirm objectives and the project plan, and develops all rules, terms, and conditions for approval. Subject matter experts are consulted on the design itself, not only on scoring, so criteria reflect what reviewers can judge. We review NIH's published criteria and recommend refinements before each round opens, with final criteria Government-approved. We establish modality-specific benchmarks, so a team proposing nanodiamond magnetometry is assessed against magnetometry sensitivity standards and one proposing quantum-enhanced spectroscopy against spectroscopy standards, rather than both against one generic rubric.

Stage 1 (Ideation) is optional, but clearing Stage 2 Milestone 1 is mandatory to reach Milestone 2. Stage 1 therefore works as a recruiting and team-formation instrument rather than a gate. Every team declares a preclinical use case and a target signal before any build, which is what makes a physicist's claim reviewable by a toxicologist. Up to 25 winners advance.

Between Stage 1 and Milestone 1, matchmaking is a deliverable, not a courtesy. Registrants declare their modality, their target signal, and what they lack; FedTech pairs across that gap and runs sessions where teams pressure-test a use case before committing. We track the registered pool and flag where one discipline is thin, so recruitment goes where the pipeline is short. Registered teams can draw on PhysioVerse, the open MPS ecosystem developed with WFIRM, and its 5,000-plus datasets, subject to Government agreement.

Milestone 1 (Initial Progress) opens to Stage 1 winners and to new entrants on equal terms, with both populations tracked separately so NCATS and BPS can see whether Stage 1 improved entry quality. It requires the sensor integrated into 2D cultures and reference fluids a receiving lab already uses, and a working data path, not a demo in the team's own rig. WFIRM can host reference experiments where a neutral bench is needed, subject to Government approval. Up to 15 winners advance.

Milestone 2 (Final Prototype) closes the evidence gap: a 3D organoid demonstration benchmarked against the classical method it would replace, carrying a prototype data package, recorded test runs, and a live session. FedTech runs them through virtual broadcasts and standardized data packages, so no team ships hardware. WFIRM's tools for interpreting NAM data, including quantum sensor signals, check results against clinical and preclinical datasets, so a sensitivity gain is tested for clinical relevance, not just instrument performance. Scoring weights calibration, limit of detection against the claimed endpoint, and reproducibility beyond a single lab. One grand prize winner and up to six runners-up.

Outreach and Participant Recruitment: A combined quantum-sensing and preclinical-toxicology solver community does not exist yet, so we recruit against two named lists rather than broadcast to one. On the quantum side: American Physical Society meetings, National Quantum Initiative centers, and the DOE quantum information science centers - Q-NEXT and C2QA at Argonne and Brookhaven, where FedTech holds active conversations, and Sandia. FedTech works this community from its Arlington, Virginia hub. On the NAMs and toxicology side: the Society of Toxicology, the NCATS tissue chip grantee community, and the NAMs standards meeting in Charlotte on December 1-2, 2026, weeks after registration opens. We recruit at HBCUs, MSIs, and EPSCoR institutions and report participation as a metric rather than asserting it.

Platform and Submission Management: The competition website runs on a custom, non-FedTech-branded URL carrying NIH and NASA identity, taking document and multimedia submissions, managing each window, and hosting the public forum. It meets Section 508 and 36 C.F.R. Part 1194, audited before each round opens. The judging record is retained a year past task order end. FedTech is the single point of contact for competitor questions and disputes.

Judging and Evaluation: A Technical Evaluation Panel scores each submission against NIH's published criteria. Quantum reviewers score the hardware, workflow and quantum-advantage categories; biomedical reviewers score the biological, measurement and data-interpretation categories; both score Team Expertise, where multidisciplinary coverage is judged. Stage 1 submissions get three reviews, one quantum and two biomedical; each Stage 2 milestone gets four, two and two. Where the panels split, FedTech convenes both chairs before recommendations reach the federal Judging Panel: a submission the hardware panel rates highly and the biology panel rates poorly is the failure mode this challenge exists to catch, and averaging would hide it. The quantum bench starts from the network FedTech built under NOIS3-039 and widens from there, recruited through QED-C and those centers. Biomedical reviewers are drawn from a WFIRM pool anchored by Anthony Atala, James Yoo, and Shay Soker and extended as volume and recusals require. External reviewers are cleared against the FAR 52.225-5 country list, submissions are screened for eligibility and scope before reaching a judge, and an SME who intends to compete in a later stage recuses from judging it.

In-Person Convening: FedTech hosts a day-and-a-half convening for 75 to 100 winners, runners-up and advisors in the DC/Bethesda area near NIH. Winners present to venture investors, SBIR program staff, and other funders FedTech brings in. FedTech drafts the agenda and run-of-show, handles venue, registration, and staffing, sends winners and runners-up travel logistics, covers its own staff travel, and delivers the post-event report. WFIRM shapes the technical content and moderates.

Reporting and Network Handoff: FedTech completes the USA.gov posting, drafts the participation agreement accepted at registration (government indemnification, $250,000 liability insurance or demonstrated financial responsibility, participant-retained IP with a Government licence option), and verifies each winner's US standing and federal-employee status before prize certification. At closeout NASA and NIH receive the network itself: a roster of specialists who would not otherwise have worked together, its engagement history, and a recommended stewardship model.

### 5.2.8.1 — Alternate Approach (optional) (2,984 / 3,000)

The base bid delivers the full Section 2.0 scope inside the $465,000 NTE. This alternate prices the additional review depth Q-1270 and Q-1277 raise, and NIH can take either tier or neither.

Basis. Q-1270 sets a floor of three reviews per Stage 1 submission and four per Stage 2 milestone submission. FedTech sizes the panel against 40 Stage 1 submissions, 25 at Milestone 1 as new entrants replace non-advancing teams, and 15 at Milestone 2. These are pricing assumptions, not participation targets. A high proportion advances out of Stage 1 by design: it is a team-formation instrument, not a gate. The binding filter is Milestone 1, which requires an integrated sensor and a working data path. That is 280 reviews, 120 quantum and 160 biomedical. The $113,985 panel line carries them: $66,520 in honoraria at $193 per quantum review and $271 per biomedical review, and $47,465 in reviewer recruitment, conflict screening against the entrant list, rubric training, reconciliation sessions, and live-session attendance.

The gap is Stage 2 depth. A Milestone 2 submission carries a prototype data package, recorded test runs, and a live session. The base honorarium buys a competent read of that package and a scored rubric, which is what the SOW requires. It does not buy an independent recomputation of the calibration and limit-of-detection claims underneath it. That recomputation is the check that separates a real quantum advantage from a well-presented one, and it is the specific thing this challenge exists to establish.

Tier 1, $519,937. Adds $57,444 across the 160 reviews in Stage 2, Milestone 1 and Milestone 2 combined, raising Stage 2 honoraria from $37,120 to $94,564, about two and a half times. Reviewers get time to work the data package, recompute calibration and limit of detection against the claimed endpoint, and write findings the Government Judging Panel can act on rather than a score it has to trust.

Tier 2, $552,396. Tier 1 plus between-milestone support at six hours a month instead of three, adding $32,459 across the 63 program months, 189 additional hours. Q-1277 describes community management, forum moderation, platform maintenance, pipeline tracking, participant coordination, and periodic check-ins across a five-year period with long gaps between milestones. The base bid meets what Q-1277 describes; this tier raises the cadence, adding proactive check-ins and deeper pipeline tracking through the long gaps between rounds.

Schedule. FedTech proposes no change. The RFTP schedule works, and every deliverable lands by December 31, 2031.

What does not change. Both tiers keep the same scope, the same team, the same milestones, and the same fixed prize purse. The only variable is how much reviewer time stands behind each award decision. If NIH takes neither tier, the base bid still meets the Q-1270 review floor at every stage. If submissions run above these assumptions, FedTech raises it at the phase gate rather than thin the review.
